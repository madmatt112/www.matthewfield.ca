## What was there before

When I incorporated Mossfoot earlier this year I put a Squarespace site up in a hurry, mostly so the company had a URL to print on an invoice. It did that job. It also looked exactly like a Squarespace template, because it was one, and it had a logo I'd drawn myself which has now been retired (with full honours).

The three services and the land acknowledgment came across to the new site. Nothing else did, on purpose.

## What's different

Everything you can see, basically. I built this one myself, the same way I built this site: a static Astro build on Cloudflare Pages, with analytics that count pages and clicks without building a profile of anybody. The contact form goes straight to my inbox. The people I want reading it are owner-operators squinting at a phone between meetings, often an older phone, so the whole thing had to be light.

The identity is new from the ground up. Paper, ink, moss, lichen, and a dab of ochre. Alegreya Sans for the headings and Atkinson Hyperlegible for the body, which is a typeface the Braille Institute designed for low-vision readers and which I picked for exactly the reader I just described. The one piece of illustration is a fiddlehead, drawn the way an old field guide would engrave it. It shows up as the _f_ in the wordmark, as the three little stage icons beside the services (coiled, rising, opened), and inside the monogram. Somewhere in the logo work it came up that Mossfoot and Matthew Field share initials, which nobody had planned and which I'm choosing to take as a sign.

Nothing on it moves or shouts, and there isn't a single all-caps label floating above a heading. That's the brand doing its job, which brings me to the name.

## Why "Mossfoot"?

There's an old saying that no moss grows under a busy person's feet. It's meant as a compliment: keep moving, gather no moss. I've always thought it had things backwards. The world has plenty of hurry in it already, and hurry is exactly where technology decisions go wrong - the rushed migration, the tool bought against a deadline, the backup nobody tested, the password policy that lives in one person's head. Moss only grows where something has stood still long enough to be worth growing on.

So the firm is named for the small, hardy & overlooked: the mosses and lichens that take the shaded side of the rock, do their slow work, and outlast nearly everything around them. (I'm also just fond of them.) That's the temperament I'm selling. Patient, and still here next year.

## What Mossfoot actually does

A lot of you found me through Tradr or through the ADHD writing, so, a refresher.

Mossfoot is a technology consultancy for organizations of roughly 10 to 75 people: owner-operated businesses, professional service firms, nonprofits, and faith communities, in Edmonton and across Canada. The person I'm usually talking to is the executive director, or the office lead, or a partner - the one the technology decisions landed on even though their real job is something else. Their systems grew up bit by bit, and nobody's quite sure what's connected to what or what it all costs. They want straight answers and a steady hand, and they would much rather prevent the fire than admire the firefighting.

There are three things I do for them, in the order they usually happen:

1. **Assessment & Roadmap.** I look at everything you run, including the quiet risks nobody's checked in years, and you leave with a written roadmap for the next 12 to 24 months.
2. **Projects & Delivery.** Migrations, rollouts, and the stalled project everyone's stopped mentioning in meetings. Planned carefully, delivered on a defined timeline, and then I stick around to make sure it holds. The first engagement under the Mossfoot name was exactly this: a primary domain migration for a nonprofit, Google Workspace and all, on time, on budget, well-scoped, and written down.
3. **Fractional Technology Leadership.** Senior technology judgment on a monthly retainer. An organization this size needs someone experienced who shows up regularly and answers plainly when something feels off. A full-time IT executive would be overkill, and a few days a month usually covers it.

Plenty of clients will run the whole arc, roadmap to retainer. Plenty won't, and you can start anywhere.

The assessment is the natural way in, and it comes in two flavours. The **Technology Assessment** looks at everything you run and how you run it, where your organization is trying to get to, and the roadmap between here and there. The **Security & Risk Assessment** is the focused version: posture, policies, practices, a risk matrix built for you, and a prioritized list of exposures with the fixes. Either way the report is yours in full to keep, and either way it's fixed-price and quoted up front. Retainers are a flat monthly amount. You see the number and what it covers before anything starts, and the invoice never carries a surprise.

How I work is on the site as four lines, and I'll stand behind them here too: in plain English, in writing, on purpose, and without the upsell. If an explanation needs jargon to sound convincing, it isn't finished yet. Decisions, changes, and credentials live in documents you own, never in one person's head. You get a recommendation and the reasoning behind it, and what you do with them is your call.

And who it's not for: if you need a 24/7 helpdesk for hundreds of staff, that's a different kind of firm, and I'll gladly point you at a good one.
