Back in the spring I had a real shot at a sales engineering job. I'd gotten through the interview, put together a demonstration solution proposal that I honestly thought was stellar, and booked a live presentation to walk them through it.

Minutes before the meeting, an access token expired. I didn't know that, of course, until I was sharing my screen with the people I was trying to impress, at which point I got to spend the demo scrambling to get the thing working again while they watched. It was... not fun.

I told that story once already, over in the [New Season post](/blog/seasons-of-life-and-announcement), as one of the reasons I stopped looking for a job. I want to tell it again, because I've been chewing on something all year and that demo turns out to be the cleanest example of it I've got: luck showed up, and I didn't have anywhere to put it.

## There's an equation for this, sort of.

The phrase "luck surface area" has been going around for a while. [Jason Roberts](https://www.codusoperandi.com/posts/increasing-your-luck-surface-area) coined it back in 2010, and the idea is that the amount of luck in your life is roughly what you're doing multiplied by how many people you're telling about it. Do interesting things in private and the second number is zero. Talk constantly and do nothing and the first one is. (I've done both.)

The video that put the phrase in my head was a designer's vlog. [EdgyKatrina](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OlBm1Tme_xs) had been laid off 2 months after the proudest project of her career, and in the meantime had developed enough travel anxiety that she was saying no to most things. So she booked a week in New York with no plan whatsoever, specifically to see whether luck would find her.

The first two nights she didn't sleep at all, and she came pretty close to changing her flight and going home. Then she noticed something: she'd been going places, but she hadn't actually told anybody she was in the city. Doing, yes. Telling, zero. So she texted her cousin, and after that the week filled itself in - museum days, dinner with a print studio she'd taken a workshop with in Manila years earlier, an aunt texting about a Broadway show that night, a print of something she'd drawn on the plane that showed up in the mail weeks later. Nothing earth-shattering. She went home feeling like she'd been living instead of just existing, which was the whole point of going.

That's the equation working as advertised. But look at _where_ her luck landed: a cousin, an aunt, a studio she'd met years before. Every bit of it landed on something that already existed before she got on the plane. She doesn't make anything of that in the video (she didn't need to), but I can't stop looking at it.

## Four months of doing without telling

For 4 months after the layoff I did nothing but apply for jobs, full time. A handful of interviews, two that went to a second round, zero offers. I've written about that stretch already too, so I'll spare you the recap.

Here's the thing about a job application, though: it's doing with the telling switched off. One recruiter sees it, maybe a hiring manager, and then the loop closes. Nobody outside that loop can see any of it, so nothing outside that loop can land on it. Four months of full-time effort with a surface area of roughly one.

And then the sales engineering interview. That _was_ luck landing, by any definition I can think of - a stage, and someone with the ability to say yes. And the thing that was supposed to catch it, a working demo, fell over at exactly the wrong minute.

Which is the variable the equation leaves out. Surface area gets luck to you. Something else entirely decides whether it sticks.

## The second variable

I wrote this on an index card a while back and I keep coming back to it: putting in the work does two things. It increases the surface area for luck to land on, _and_ it prepares a substrate that will catch, hold, and grow the spark when it hits.

(Beekeeper here, so bear with the gardening metaphor.) Seeds land everywhere, all the time. They only grow on ground that somebody turned over before they got there.

The demo had plenty of surface area. What it didn't have was a substrate: a run-through that same morning, a deck of screenshots for when the live thing dies, &c. Any one of those and the exact same luck would have stuck.

There's a companion card, which is more of a question than a claim: _am I putting in what I need to, so that the things I want come out the other side?_ Same idea from the input end. If something good showed up today, is there anything here for it to grab onto?

## What the launch caught, and what it dropped

On August 27 I posted about Tradr on LinkedIn. I had 264 followers at the time. The post got 137,502 impressions and reached 92,810 people, 99% of them outside my network (I still don't entirely understand how that happened, and I'm not sure LinkedIn does either). 898 of those people clicked through to my profile. 22 followed. 71 saved the post for later.

The substrate caught a good chunk of that. Anyone who clicked landed on a working registration page, a docs site, a public repo, and a release pipeline that shipped a fix 5 days later. The first bug report came in within a day, from a fella I last worked with at NextDigital, and it was a real one: the onboarding walkthrough was broken, and my staging environment had somehow hidden that from me. That bug got found because the post existed. It got fixed in 5 days because the pipeline did.

The substrate dropped a lot too. 92,810 people reached and 22 new followers is a pretty rough conversion rate. There was no newsletter line in that post, and nothing on my site yet for a stranger to fall into and stay. The audience part of the substrate just hadn't been built. The luck landed on it anyway, and slid right off.

The same week I posted the same product to r/opensource. Zero upvotes, one comment calling it AI slop. Same substrate, different surface. You don't get to pick which surface the luck lands on, which is why another one of my cards says _more and smaller bets_: I don't know which ones will work yet, and the outside world will tell me.

## The part where I argue with two people at once

One more card, and then I'll stop quoting myself: _visioning is tuning our receptor paths to specific things. It doesn't mystically, magically create those things out of nothing._

I think there are two kinds of people who get luck wrong, and they get it wrong in mirror image. The hustle version treats surface area as the whole game: post more, ship more, and the numbers will take care of themselves. The manifestation version treats wanting as the whole game: get clear on what you want, really feel it, and it'll come to you.

Honestly, both are about half right. Wanting something does change what you notice when it flies past - that's real, and it's a nervous-system thing. Doing things in public does change how often something flies past at all. Neither one has any say in whether it sticks. That's the substrate, and it's the only one of the three you build entirely on your own time, before anything has shown up.

## So, what am I doing about it?

I gave myself 6 months to make self-employment sustainably pay the bills, which puts the clock at February. The bets are more and smaller. Field Notes (the newsletter this essay goes out in) is the audience substrate I didn't have on launch day. The Tradr repo stays public so that contributor luck has somewhere to land. And every Friday I sit down and write out what happened that week, which turns out to be the tuning: what landed, and what slid off.

I don't know which of these is going to be the one. I'm fairly sure that's the point.
